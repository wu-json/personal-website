Today marks my 1.5th year living in San Francisco.

It feels like ages since I moved here from the peaceful suburbs of Northern Georgia to chase my lifelong dream of building the final AI harness for B2B SaaS.

> Ok fine... maybe not lifelong. I was truly, very excited though.

After working remote for the past five years, this would be the first in-person job I ever took in the United States, and man was it in-person. We would work from a beautiful waterside hacker house in the Marina seven days a week, and often share drinks and stupid stories with each other on holidays. While exhausting, I bonded with some amazing people under incredibly peculiar circumstances, and gained enough stories to fill a short book.

**I have no regrets.**

Regardless of your opinions on 996, it is undoubtedly a breeding ground for lore. A lot of this stems from just spending more time with the team. More time means more things, some good things, and some bad things.

Despite my years in startups, this was the first time I experienced real Silicon Valley startup culture. While I will certainly write about my hacker house experience at some point, I would like to spend my mental energy here reflecting on a much more perplexing source of cultural whiplash I experienced during this move — the art of the transactional relationship.

## Coffee Chat

In Georgia, grabbing a coffee meant sipping on a diluted americano in the brutal summer heat talking to dear Amber about how her kids were doing in school.

In San Francisco, it took me a few meetups before I started to realize that _I was the coffee._

## Grind, Pour Over, and Extract

I accepted a coffee chat with a VC that I did not know very well within the first couple of weeks of moving to the city, and thought nothing to myself besides, "wow the people here must be very friendly."

We met at an oddly sterile looking cafe near my neighborhood, and after ordering my drink he graciously paid for it in full using his Ramp card, and made sure I knew about it. He didn't get a drink himself, because coffee in the afternoon was bad for sleep.

After what felt like one minute of small talk, he immediately began to mine me for information about my company. What customers do you serve? What is your annual revenue? Do you think the team culture is robust?

I'm an extremely introverted person (Quiet by Susan Cain was my personal reading book in middle school), so it's very rare that I find myself wanting more small talk. In these conversations however, it felt so unnatural that I would crave it like a stray dog that had not seen fresh meat in ages.

Another frustrating observation was the pushiness. If I seemed uncomfortable answering something, he would dance around the boundaries, and toy with verbal wizardry to snatch the information he wanted without wrecking his delusional view of his own rapport. A thief in the Blue Bottle perhaps.

After the unusual conversation, he gave me a hug and left in a Waymo. As I stepped out the door to leave myself, I did a quick turn to look at the other customers in the shop.

For some reason, I feel like I wasn't the only bean going into the pour-over today.

> Not all VCs and founders are like this, even if a lot of them are. I have friends in these roles that don't get anything out of me. If anything, I probably make them less productive.

## Stay Safe Out There

My family moved out of Georgia while I was working in San Francisco, so I lost my main reason to go back to the dear old town next to the funny looking water tower. To the cows in the ranch in front of our neighborhood, I miss you!

During my 3 years in Georgia, I was surprised to hear so many Georgians say goodbye to me with the phrase:

"Stay safe out there!"

Back when I was in the Northeast, no one ever said this to me, even when I lived in areas where I probably needed this blessing the most. That said, I don't think I truly understood the nuance of this farewell until now.

Where I lived in Georgia, people would talk to you and help you out for no reason. The trainers at my local gym would give me tips for free just because I was a regular, and my dad even had an instance where he struck up a conversation with some carpenters in the Home Depot parking lot because they thought his truck was cool.

When a Georgian tells you to stay safe out there, there's a hint of "I'm not sure whether I'll ever see you again" sprinkled in. Despite that, they still choose to be kind and embrace you because Southern Hospitality is not an investment, it's just being human.

## Trojan Cows

I'm not saying that you should expect Southern Hospitality from your investors, nor am I saying that you should provide Southern Hospitality to your investors. In fact, they might find it kind of weird if you do either of these things.

The southern farewell simply helped me understand the phenomenon behind why I found some of my interactions in SF so weird. I call it the Trojan Cow.

Trojan Cows feign hospitality. They get you to meet them on the grounds of friendship, a mutual connection, or just checking in on you to see how you're doing (personally of course). Then the moment you're in person, they pop out of the wooden getup, with a clean sword funded by the company card pointed right at your neck.

The problem is the lack of authenticity.

Transactional relationships are fine on their own, as long as everyone is out of the cow and unarmed. Not everyone has the time nor the emotional energy to treat everyone like family and that's ok. That is expected in a world where social networks are as extensive as they have ever been.

When it comes to business, ethos is a currency that gets devalued the more you use it. At some point, the person on the other end throws your empathetic coins on the ground and asks how much they are really worth. If you choose to be transactional, rely on incentives instead. It keeps the relationship more robust, and doesn't create these awkward emotional dynamics.

If your goal is real human connection, then blind yourself to the lust of incentives, otherwise you'll find yourself waking up in a wooden cow. If incentives are human physics, then breaking them produces miracles.

I still choose to believe in miracles.
